When A Model Decides Not To Answer (2017-2026)

In this episode we unpack a Nature Machine Intelligence paper published on 7 September 2026 by Dharshan Kumaran, Nathaniel Daw, Simon Osindero, Petar Velickovic and Viorica Patraucean of Google DeepMind, with Daw at Princeton. You can already squeeze a confidence number out of a language model, but nobody had shown the model itself uses that signal to decide whether to answer or stay quiet, rather than the number being a bystander that happens to correlate with being right. Across a four-phase paradigm on 1,000 SimpleQA questions and four models, the authors close that gap causally, and argue that abstention runs through a two-stage architecture: a rich internal confidence representation, then a threshold policy that reads it.

We walk through the paradigm in order, from GPT-4o's 63.7 percent baseline accuracy to the moment an abstain option appears and calibrated confidence outpredicts question difficulty, retrieval accessibility and sentence embeddings by roughly ten to one, with an implicit threshold near 77 percent. The causal test is activation steering in Gemma 3 27B, where injecting high or low confidence at intermediate layers swings abstention from 66.5 percent to 7.0 percent, a 59.5 point move that mediation analysis traces mostly to confidence redistribution rather than a shifted decision policy. We pair the result with Fleming and Daw's second-order Bayesian framework, which supplies its Stage 1 and Stage 2, and with four papers that pull against it: the same team's earlier finding that LLM confidence carries a choice-supportive bias and hypersensitivity to contradiction, Meta FAIR's AbstentionBench showing abstention is unsolved and that reasoning fine-tuning makes it 24 percent worse, Arditi and colleagues' result that refusal is mediated by a single one-dimensional direction, and Steyvers and colleagues' calibration gap, where users overestimate an answer's accuracy no matter what the model knows. We close on the finding we keep returning to, that the model's spoken self-rating is worse at telling right from wrong than its own log probabilities and still independently predicts when it stays quiet, which means the confidence numbers we can read are lossy shadows of whatever the model is actually using.
