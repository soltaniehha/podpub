Turing-like Tests for Language and Vision Demonstrate a Narrowing Gap Between Humans and AI (October 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new Nature Human Behaviour paper from Mengmi Zhang at Nanyang Technological University, senior author Gabriel Kreiman at Boston Children's Hospital and Harvard Medical School, and 21 co-authors, published on 26 October 2026 after circulating as an arXiv preprint since November 2022. The question is a practical one: can you still tell whether an answer came from a person or a machine? Across 72,191 Turing-like tests with 1,916 human judges, the answer is barely, because AI is close to passing as human in both language and vision, while simple AI judges spot it far better than people do.

We walk through the six tasks (image captioning, word association, conversation, colour estimation, object detection and eye-movement attention prediction), the 636 human participants and 37 AI agents from GPT-3, BlenderBot, BLIP and GIT to LLaMA-3-70B and ChatGPT-4o, and the imitation detectability score, where 0.5 means a perfect imitator. We cover a linear SVM judge on OpenAI embeddings that beats human judges, the live chat version where judges caught GPT-3.5-Turbo every time, and the finding that human-likeness barely tracks BLEU and CIDEr scores. Along the way we set it against Turing's 1950 imitation game, Jones and Bergen's two Turing tests of GPT-4 and GPT-4.5, Pagan and colleagues' computational Turing test, and Chollet's case for measuring intelligence differently, and close on phishing, deepfakes and a future where people may have to prove they are human.
