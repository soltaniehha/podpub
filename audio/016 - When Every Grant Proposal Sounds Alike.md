When Every Grant Proposal Sounds Alike (2024–2026)

In this episode we unpack a new PNAS study published on 11 August 2026 by Yifan Qian, Zhe Wen, Alexander C. Furnas, Yue Bai, Erzhuo Shao and Dashun Wang of Northwestern University's Center for Science of Science and Innovation, and we set it against the fight that broke out the same week over whether any of this can be measured at all. The Northwestern team got hold of something researchers almost never see: the confidential NSF and NIH proposal submissions of two large US R1 universities from 2021 to 2025, funded, unfunded and pending alike, paired with the full public population of 57,000 NSF and 74,000 NIH awards. Their finding is that large language models are already inside the federal funding pipeline, and that the proposals leaning on them hardest are the ones that most closely resemble whatever the agency funded the year before.

We walk through how they know it: Liang et al.'s distributional detection framework, which estimates the fraction of LLM-modified sentences in an abstract by calibrating real pre-ChatGPT 2021 grant text against GPT-3.5-turbo-0125 rewrites of the same abstracts, and which shows adoption jumping from 2023 into a bimodal split between researchers who barely touch these tools and researchers who lean on them hard. We cover the central result, measured as SPECTER2 embedding distance to the previous year's funded work: within a single investigator compared against themselves, heavier LLM involvement means about five percentile points less distinctiveness at NSF and four at NIH, and a placebo in which an LLM rewrites 2021 abstracts without changing the science barely moves the needle, which suggests the convergence is substantive rather than cosmetic. We follow the agency split that makes the paper strange, with no relationship at NSF between LLM use and either funding success or later output, against a roughly four percentage point rise in NIH funding probability and five percent more publications that evaporates once you restrict to top five percent hit papers. Then we turn to the dispute: Kusumegi et al.'s Science paper reporting output gains of 23.7 to 89.3 percent for LLM adopters, the PNAS Comment from Thomas Renault, Antonin Bergeaud and Clément Bosquet arguing that dating adoption to the first detector hit mechanically manufactures that rise, and the original authors' August 2026 reply showing the artifact is real but too small to explain their result. We close on Hao, Xu, Li and Evans in Nature, whose 41.3 million paper study finds AI-augmented scientists publishing 3.02 times more and drawing 4.84 times more citations while the collective range of topics contracts, and on the exploration versus exploitation question this leaves for agencies that are supposed to be funding the risky work.
