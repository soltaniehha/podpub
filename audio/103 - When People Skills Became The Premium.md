The Growing Importance of Social Skills in the Labor Market (November 2017)

In this episode we unpack David Deming's Quarterly Journal of Economics paper, the foundational work on why the labor market came to reward working well with others. The argument: as computers took over routine work, jobs that need people to adapt to one another grew, and social skill became a way of lowering the cost of coordination.

We walk through his Ricardian model of teamwork as workers "trading tasks", the O*NET and Census evidence that social skill-intensive jobs grew 11.8 percentage points as a share of employment from 1980 to 2012 while high math, low social jobs shrank about 3.3 points, and the NLSY79 and NLSY97 comparison showing social skills becoming a much stronger predictor of full-time work. We test how it held up with Weinberger's cohort evidence, Autor on Polanyi's paradox, Deming and Kahn on nearly 45 million job ads, the Weidmann and Deming team player experiment, and Althoff and Reichardt's 2026 model projecting that AI compresses these returns. We close on what Deming got right, what generative AI complicates, and what universities should teach if machines start lowering coordination costs for everyone.
