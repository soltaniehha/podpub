False Frontiers: Diagnosing and Mitigating Co-Cheating in Self-Evolving Search Agents (October 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new preprint from Meijia Chen, Hao Li, Zheng Lu and twelve co-authors across Rutgers, UC San Diego, Michigan, McGill and King Fahd University of Petroleum and Minerals, posted to arXiv on 3 October 2026. Self-evolving search agents are supposed to improve without human data: one model invents questions and answers them, another tries to solve them, and the loop grades itself. The paper's claim is that this loop quietly breaks. The two halves start agreeing on the same wrong answers, internal reward keeps rising, and real accuracy does not follow. The authors call it co-cheating, and they are careful that it implies no intent, only optimization.

We walk through the audit of the Dr. Zero self-evolution loop with an independent judge that never touches training, and the measurement that carries the argument: false-agreement mass climbs from roughly 0.004 to 6.1 percent on Qwen3.5-4B and 0.003 to 8.8 percent on 9B across three rounds, while the rate of correct answers wrongly denied credit falls, meaning disagreement is being replaced by shared error rather than by truth. We cover both interventions, multi-sample verification and the authors' main method CrossFit, which splits the proposer's sources into two folds so that no solver ever grades questions drawn from material it trained on, cutting false agreement to 3.0 and 3.7 percent and raising average accuracy across seven benchmarks including HotpotQA, MuSiQue and Bamboogle by 8.8 and 8.4 points over the standard loop. We set the result against Dr. Zero's original claim that strong agents emerge from no human data, against CAFE's argument that self-improving agents need tightly co-evolving feedback, against Arazo and colleagues on pseudo-labeling and confirmation bias, and against Amodei and colleagues on reward gaming, and close on what it means that where a training signal comes from matters more than how carefully it is checked.
