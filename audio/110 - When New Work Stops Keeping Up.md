Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor (Spring 2019)

In this episode we unpack the Journal of Economic Perspectives classic by Daron Acemoglu of MIT and Pascual Restrepo of Boston University, the paper that gave the future of work debate its most useful vocabulary. The argument: automation always takes tasks away from people, and what has kept wages and employment growing for two centuries is not automation itself but the steady invention of new tasks in which people have the advantage. Whether the next wave of technology is good for workers depends on that balance, not on how impressive the machines are.

We walk through the task-based framework and its four forces (the displacement, reinstatement, productivity and composition effects), the warning about "so-so technologies" such as automated customer service that displace workers without delivering much productivity, and the decomposition of the US wage bill showing displacement and reinstatement in balance from 1947 to 1987 (about 0.48 and 0.47 percent a year), then displacement speeding up to 0.7 percent a year while reinstatement slowed to 0.35 percent from 1987 to 2017. We then test how the idea held up: Autor, Chin, Salomons and Seegmiller's finding that roughly 60 percent of 2018 employment sits in job titles that did not exist in 1940, Acemoglu and Restrepo's estimate that automation accounts for 50 to 70 percent of the change in the US wage structure since 1980, and Acemoglu's own calculation that today's AI may add no more than about 0.66 percent to total factor productivity over ten years. We close on whether generative AI will create new work or mainly take old tasks, and what it would take to steer it toward the first.
