When Correlation Learns To Say Cause (2016-2026)

In this episode we unpack a new Nature Human Behaviour paper from Calvin Isch, Timothy Dörr, Neil Fasching, Grace Jennings and Duncan J. Watts at the University of Pennsylvania, published online 24 August 2026. They ask how often social science says "causes" when its design can only support "is associated with", and they find it in 46.3% of 194,631 cross-sectional articles, a rate that sat near 20% from 1980 to 2000 and then rose almost threefold to over 60% by 2024. We pair it with five earlier papers that explain why that language survives, why readers believe it anyway, and what changes now that a language model sits between the paper and the person reading it.

We walk through the three-step human-in-the-loop workflow behind the count, a GPT-4o prompt validated against 200 expert-coded abstracts and distilled into a fine-tuned BERT classifier, and the numbers it produces: business highest at 84% and sociology lowest at 46%, more causal language in higher-impact journals than lower ones, and unhedged claims concentrated in introductions and discussions rather than methods. We cover the preregistered experiment with 1,105 readers, where methodological labels and associational rewrites reduce causal inference without ever eliminating it, and the machine experiments across GPT-4.1, GPT-5.1, GPT-5.4, DeepSeek Chat-V3.2 and Claude Sonnet 4.6, where a plain summarization prompt turns hedged abstracts into unhedged causal claims and a single instruction to be careful pulls the rate back to about 5%. Tal Yarkoni's generalizability crisis gives us the gap between verbal hypotheses and the statistics meant to test them; Samuel Gershman and Tomer Ullman show experimentally that people hear cause in the words "is associated with"; Paul Smaldino and Richard McElreath model the incentives that select for the habit rather than punish it; Fabrizio Gilardi and colleagues established the annotation method that makes a study this size possible; and Uwe Peters and Benjamin Chin-Yee, testing ten models on 4,900 summaries, found newer models generalizing worse, which is precisely what the new paper's GPT-5.4 result complicates. We close on the reflexive bind: the tool that strips the hedges out is the same one now proposed to police them.
