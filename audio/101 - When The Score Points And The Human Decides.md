Predictive Risk Scores in the Public Sector (August 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new NBER working paper from Jason Baron and Arkadev Ghosh of Duke and Richard Lombardo of Harvard, a pre-registered randomized trial of what happens when a machine prediction sits beside professional judgment and nobody is told what to do. The argument: a simple risk score, built only from information already in the file, can change which cases get attention and improve outcomes while people keep full discretion.

We walk through the Northampton County, Pennsylvania experiment, where 4,752 child referrals were randomized on one thing, whether supervisors could see a five-level XGBoost complexity score. Foster care placement nearly doubled from a 1.3 percent base, the changes concentrated in the Very High group, and three-month re-referrals fell 15 percent, with no evidence of wider racial disparities. We test that against Baron, Dobbie and colleagues on Michigan's multistage system, where no hotline screener beats the algorithm but a third of investigators do, Grimon and Mills on screening, Stevenson and Doleac on Virginia judges, and Hoffman, Kahn and Li on managers who overrule job tests. We close on the suggestive and fragile finding that the gains sit with the stronger investigators, and on what universities should teach people who will be handed the score before they have built the judgment.
