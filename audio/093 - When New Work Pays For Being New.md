What Makes New Work Different from More Work? (March 2026)

In this episode we unpack a March 2026 NBER working paper by David Autor and Caroline Chin of MIT, Anna Salomons of Tilburg and Utrecht, and Bryan Seegmiller of Northwestern's Kellogg School, which asks what actually happens when technology creates new kinds of jobs. The argument: new work offsets automation not just by adding more jobs but by creating new domains of human expertise that are scarce at first and pay a premium, which is why economies that keep inventing new work, and keep training people for it, hold on to labor's share of income.

We walk through the data, person-level job write-ins from the 1940 and 1950 Census Complete Count files and the confidential American Community Survey for 2011 to 2023, matched to new titles in the Census occupation index, which show that 18.3 percent of recent workers hold job titles introduced since 1970. We cover who gets new work (younger and more educated workers, even inside the same occupation), the wage premium it carries (largest for technology-linked titles, about 9 log points for those added between 2000 and 2018) and how that premium fades as titles age, and the World War II plant-building program that seeded new work where it was sited. We set it against Lin's original measure of new work, Atalay and colleagues' job-ad evidence on how tasks change inside job titles, Feigenbaum and Gross on what happened to telephone operators when AT&T automated switching, and Humlum and Vestergaard's 2026 Danish evidence that chatbot users are already taking on new tasks in content generation, AI oversight and AI integration with no measurable change yet in earnings or hours. We close on whether generative AI will generate its own wave of expertise-demanding new work, and who will be positioned to do it.
