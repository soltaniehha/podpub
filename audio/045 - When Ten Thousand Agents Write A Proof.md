When Ten Thousand Agents Write A Proof (1934-2026)

In this episode we unpack the two mathematics papers OpenAI released on 8 September 2026, "Finite time blowup for Navier-Stokes" and "Finite time blowup for the Euler equation", produced by roughly 10,000 coordinating AI agents running on an unreleased model, which reached their resolution in about 88 hours and then had it machine-checked in Lean 4 in a further 17 hours. The author line on both papers reads only "OPENAI"; no human is named. Read against Charles Fefferman's official Clay Mathematics Institute problem statement, the claim needs stating carefully: the Navier-Stokes theorem establishes alternatives (C) and (D), breakdown in the presence of a smooth compactly supported force, and not the unforced global regularity question of (A) and (B) that most people mean by the Millennium Problem. OpenAI says it does not intend to claim the prize.

We walk through what was actually proved and the asterisk attached to it, the self-similar vortex that spirals inward and elongates while its kinetic energy stays bounded, and why the Euler companion result, which needs no forcing at all, may be the deeper of the two. We spend most of the episode on the AI question rather than the fluid mechanics: why the Lean formalization is the load-bearing part, since a machine-checked certificate can be verified without trusting whoever wrote it, and how Prove2Me and Anthropic's autonomous Lean formalization of Fermat's Last Theorem in the same month place formal verification at the centre of AI-generated mathematics. We trace the lineage back to AlphaProof, the AlphaZero-style agent that reached silver-medal standard at the 2024 International Mathematical Olympiad inside Lean, and ask whether moving from competition problems with known answers to an open research problem is a difference of degree or of kind. We give the humans their due, since the paper's own introduction builds on Cordoba and Martinez-Zoroa's amplification strategy and Fefferman has called them the heroes, and we do not soften the priority dispute, in which Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpoge announced Euler results twelve hours earlier and have raised the question of whether OpenAI's agents benefited from in-progress work conducted on OpenAI's own models. We close on the Discovery Certification Protocol and the split it exposes: for the truth of the theorem the Lean proof is close to an ideal certificate, and for attribution, novelty and provenance it certifies nothing at all.
