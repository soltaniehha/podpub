Human Learning Is an Understudied but Promising Lever for Boosting Human–AI Synergy (September 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new PNAS paper from Julian Berger, Stefan Herzog, Ralph Hertwig, Ralf Kurvers and colleagues at the Max Planck Institute for Human Development. It takes on one of the most quoted findings about working with AI, that humans and AI together tend to do worse than the better of the two alone, and argues that the finding says as much about how the experiments were designed as about people. Most studies never let participants learn whether the AI was right.

We walk through their re-coding of all 74 studies in Vaccaro, Almaatouq and Malone's 2024 Nature Human Behaviour meta-analysis, where the typical experiment ran one session of about 30 trials with no practice and no outcome feedback, and only 10 studies ever showed people the correct answer. We cover their robust Bayesian meta-regressions (RoBMA), which tie feedback to tentatively higher synergy and show AI explanations turning clearly harmful without feedback but positive with it. We set this against Bansal and colleagues' finding that explanations mostly raise blind acceptance, Hullman, Kale and Hartline's decision-theoretic critique of underspecified human decision experiments, and the same Max Planck group's hybrid confirmation tree, which skips advice-taking entirely and aggregates independent human and AI judgments. We close on whether workplaces and classrooms should teach people to verify AI, or build workflows that do not depend on it.
