The Future of Employment: How Susceptible Are Jobs to Computerisation? (September 2013; published January 2017)

In this episode we unpack the most quoted number in the future of work debate: Carl Benedikt Frey and Michael Osborne's estimate, from the Oxford Martin School, that 47 percent of US employment was at high risk of computerisation, first circulated as a 2013 working paper and published in Technological Forecasting and Social Change in 2017. The argument: machine learning and mobile robotics let computers move beyond routine, rule-based work, so the limit on automation is no longer routineness but three engineering bottlenecks, perception and manipulation, creative intelligence and social intelligence.

We walk through how the estimate was built, from 70 occupations hand-labelled as automatable or not at an Oxford engineering workshop, to nine O*NET variables that stand in for the three bottlenecks, to a Gaussian process classifier that scores 702 occupations, along with the predicted two waves of computerisation, the strong negative link between automation risk and both wages and education, and the authors' own caveat that they estimate what is technically possible, not how many jobs will go. We then test how it held up: Arntz, Gregory and Zierahn's task-level reanalysis that cuts the share to about 9 percent across 21 OECD countries, Autor's case for why there are still so many jobs, Eloundou and colleagues' finding that language models reach higher-wage cognitive work, Frey and Osborne's own 2023 reappraisal for the generative AI era, and the Chicago Fed's 2026 check of the forecast against what happened to jobs and wages. We close on what a decade of hindsight teaches about forecasting and why risk scores are better read as signs of restructuring than as countdowns.
