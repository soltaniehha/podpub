How Much Agent Is Your Agent (1996–2026)

In this episode we unpack a paper published in Nature on 12 August 2026 by Atoosa Kasirzadeh of Carnegie Mellon University and Iason Gabriel of Google DeepMind, who argue that we are trying to regulate AI agents without any agreed way of saying how much of an agent a given system actually is. The phrase "AI agent" now covers a chess engine, a chatbot, a browser-driving assistant and a self-driving car, and governance frameworks that treat them as one category will either smother the harmless ones or wave the dangerous ones through. Their proposal is to stop asking whether something is an agent and start measuring how much of one it is, along four dimensions, and then to draw the result as that system's "agentic profile."

We walk through the four dimensions and the gradations that make them usable: autonomy from A.0, entirely dependent on its principal, to A.5, all tasks with no oversight at all, a scale lifted deliberately from the Society of Automotive Engineers' six levels of driving automation; efficacy built as a matrix rather than a line, crossing the degree of causal impact from observation-only to comprehensive against whether the environment is simulated, mediated or physical, with Klyubin, Polani and Nehaniv's empowerment measure offered as one way to quantify it; goal complexity from GC.0, no goal and therefore not an agent, to GC.5, where the system generates its own goal structures and interprets underspecified objectives, with METR's work on how long a task a model can reliably finish as the practical yardstick; and generality from a single speciality to a fully general system, built on Morris and colleagues' Levels of AGI. We then read out the four worked profiles that make the framework concrete, and they are not where intuition puts them: AlphaGo scores A.3, E.1, GC.2, G.1, more autonomous than people assume but sealed inside a board; standalone ChatGPT-3.5 lands at A.2, E.2, GC.3, G.3, broad but almost powerless on the world; Claude 3.5 Sonnet with tool use reaches A.3, E.3, GC.4, G.3, jumping three of four dimensions on scaffolding alone; and a Waymo vehicle sits at A.4, E.4, GC.4, G.2, the narrow one that can actually kill someone. That yields the paper's sharpest claim, that an agentic profile is a property of a deployment rather than a model, so adding tools, memory or reasoning to the same base system makes it a different agent, and leaves open the individuation question the authors cannot yet answer: when does one agent become another. We set this against Franklin and Graesser, who asked the original version of the question in 1996 and answered it with a hard binary, against the working definitions from Shavit and colleagues at OpenAI and the harm taxonomy from Chan and colleagues, and against the one source here that genuinely disagrees, Mitchell, Ghosh, Luccioni and Pistilli at Hugging Face, who argue that full autonomy should be ruled out entirely rather than graded. We close on what changes if regulators adopt profiles, and on the authors' own concession that no validated metric exists yet for any of the four dimensions.
