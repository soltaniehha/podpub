Group Size Effects and Collective Misalignment in LLM Multi-Agent Systems (August 2026)

In this episode we unpack a paper published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences on 18 August 2026 by Ariel Flint Ashery, Luca Maria Aiello, Romualdo Pastor-Satorras and Andrea Baronchelli, working across City St George's University of London, the IT University of Copenhagen and the Pioneer Centre for AI, and the Universitat Politecnica de Catalunya in Barcelona. Almost everything we know about groups of AI agents comes from comparing one agent against a group of some arbitrarily chosen size. This paper asks the question that comparison skips: how big is the group? Sweeping population size from two agents up to a million, the authors find that group size is not a neutral parameter. It changes whether a population of language model agents develops a collective bias, how strong that bias gets, and in some cases which way it points. The version we work from is the authors' preprint, posted in October 2025 under the same title; the peer-reviewed paper appeared in PNAS two days before this episode.

We walk through the naming game the agents play, where two of them at a time propose one of two words, score points only if they match, and remember nothing but their last five encounters, and through the trick that makes million-agent simulation feasible, which is caching a probability policy read straight off the model's logits for every possible memory state. The agents are built from Microsoft Phi-4, OpenAI GPT-4o, Qwen QwQ-32B and Meta Llama 3.1 70B Instruct, coordinating on eleven bias-sensitive word pairs taken from CrowS-Pairs. The central finding splits collective bias into three distinct forms: amplification, where interaction sharpens a preference the agents already had; induction, where genuinely neutral agents produce a biased population out of nothing; and reversal, where the group settles on the word its own members individually disfavoured. All three are invisible to any single-agent audit. On top of that, group size drives the effect non-linearly. GPT-4o populations converge on one word in a pair 62.6 percent of the time at two agents and 100 percent of the time at a thousand. Llama needs six agents before a reversal appears at all, and Qwen still shows finite-size effects at ten thousand. A mean-field theory maps the game onto a reaction-diffusion process and shows that above a critical size the noise washes out and the outcome becomes predictable in advance. Against this we set the same group's Science Advances study that first found collective bias in LLM populations, the twenty-year-old statistical-physics naming game it is built on, the human experiments that made that model credible, and a direct disagreement from De Marzo, Castellano and Garcia, who found coordination getting harder rather than easier as groups grow. We close on why that disagreement turns out to be about what the agents can see, and on the authors' warning that a system audited at one size can misbehave at another.
