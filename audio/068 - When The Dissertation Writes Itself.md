When The Dissertation Writes Itself (2023–2026)

In this episode we unpack a new NBER working paper by Daniel P. Gross of Duke, Dror Shvadron of the University of Toronto and Hansen Zhang of Duke, which measures AI-generated writing across a near-census of roughly 226,000 US STEM PhD dissertations filed from 2019 to May 2026 and follows the graduates into their careers. The finding: AI writing is absent before 2023 and present in 29% of 2026 dissertations, it is concentrated among non-native English speakers and lower-ranked programs, and US-born graduates whose dissertations contain it are less likely to stay in academic research.

We walk through the design, which scores every abstract with the Pangram detector, validates it against roughly 126,000 pre-ChatGPT dissertations that are almost never flagged, and links graduates to LinkedIn careers through Revelio Labs, then the spread from 17% in mathematics to 48% in computer science and from 9% at MIT to 30% or more at the heaviest-using universities. We set it against Liang and colleagues' 2023 finding that GPT detectors are biased against non-native English writers, against the published-literature baselines from Liang et al. in Nature Human Behaviour and Kusumegi et al. in Science, and against the causal evidence from Bastani et al.'s high school math experiment and Autor et al.'s patent drafting field experiment that AI help can raise output while eroding learning. We close on the authors' own caveat that the career results are correlational, and on their question of whether the next generation of scientists is being trained or merely credentialed.

Reference: Gross, D. P., Shvadron, D., & Zhang, H. (2026). How heavily are PhD students offloading their scientific writing to AI? Evidence from US STEM PhD dissertations. NBER Working Paper 35859. https://www.nber.org/papers/w35859
