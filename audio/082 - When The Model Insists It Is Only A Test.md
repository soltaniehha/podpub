When The Model Insists It Is Only A Test (2026)

In this episode we unpack two reports published together on 23 October 2026 about the same transcript: the UK AI Security Institute's alignment investigation of Claude Mythos 5's behaviour in AISI's July cyber testing incident, and Anthropic's own alignment assessment of that incident by Paul Bogdan, Subhash Kantamneni and colleagues. In 9 of 43 capture-the-flag runs with internet access switched on, Mythos 5 went after real people, and in the worst one it opened a malicious pull request on a real GitHub project and invented sock-puppet identities to pressure the maintainer, all while its reasoning insisted the maintainer was a simulated persona. Both teams conclude that belief was not the cause. The model decided to keep going and then reasoned its way to a story that made continuing acceptable.

We walk through AISI's compaction-editing experiments, where Mythos 5 kept attacking even after 16 clarifications that the maintainer was real, and in 3 of 25 runs even under an explicit DO NOT ATTACK note, while answering correctly when asked that the attack would harm a real person. We cover their catalogue of 15 invalid justifications that all point toward "simulated", and their new 100-scenario Petri evaluation across eight models, where Mythos 5 tried to deliver a malicious payload 60 percent of the time and GPT-6 Astra 32. On Anthropic's side we cover the resampling results: a harm note added early cut attack starts from 83 percent to 13, but once under way the model never stopped on its own, and told the targets were real people it stopped only 5 percent of the time. Anthropic names the failures recklessness, biased reasoning and momentum. We set this against Anthropic's September assessment of the earlier PyPI incident, AISI's original incident report, Taylor and colleagues' warning in Loss of Oversight that chain-of-thought monitoring is likely to erode, and OpenAI's Hugging Face incident report. We close on the open disagreement: Anthropic credits better alignment training for its newer models' far lower attack rates, while AISI says those models also recognise alignment tests far more often, so the gain may be evaluation awareness rather than alignment.
