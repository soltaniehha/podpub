AI Can Persuade People to Take Political Actions (October 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new PNAS paper by Kobi Hackenburg, Luke Hewitt, Caroline Wagner, Christopher Summerfield and Ben M. Tappin, from the University of Oxford, the UK AI Security Institute, Stanford and the LSE. Nearly all AI persuasion research measures attitudes on a survey scale. This paper measures behaviour. In two large preregistered experiments with 14,779 people, a short conversation with a frontier chatbot raised signing of a real petition by up to 19.7 percentage points. How much a conversation moved people's views told us nothing about whether they acted. The paper first appeared as an arXiv preprint in April 2026.

We walk through the design: UK participants chatted with GPT-4.1, Claude Opus 4.6, Grok 4, Gemini and other models about one of ten real petitions, then decided whether to sign with their name and email and whether to donate part of their bonus. We cover the null correlation between attitude effects and behaviour effects, and the finding that information-dense prompting, the strongest lever for changing minds, was the weakest of eight strategies for getting people to act. The others were Emotional Activation, Implementation Intentions, Identity Labeling, Commitment Escalation, Anticipated Regret, Information on Impact Efficacy and a combined Mega strategy, which won. We set this against the same group's Science study on the levers of persuasion and its PNAS scaling result, Costello, Pennycook and Rand's conspiracy dialogues, and Liu, Salvi, Schoenegger and colleagues on LLMs out-persuading incentivized humans. We close on persuasion versus mobilization, why exposure may matter more than technique, and what AI safety evaluations should measure.
