When Crisis Footage Stops Being Proof (2020-2026)

In this episode we unpack RA-Bench, a benchmark released on arXiv on 14 August 2026 by a 36-author collaboration led by Shuo Liang, Yixing Ma and Pengfei Zhou, spanning the National University of Singapore, UC Berkeley, ETH Zurich, Stanford, Carnegie Mellon, Zhejiang, Renmin and a dozen more institutions. It asks a narrow and uncomfortable question: if someone fabricates video of a war, a wildfire, a pandemic response or an infrastructure failure, can anything reliably catch it? The answer is no, on three separate fronts, and the five older papers we read alongside it explain how the field arrived at the opposite expectation. Detection research was founded on a promising result about generalization, extended by a claim of universality, and reassured by evidence that humans and machines fail differently enough to cover for each other. RA-Bench tests all three of those beliefs against real crisis footage and finds each one weaker than advertised.

We walk through how RA-Bench is built, anchoring 1,830 human-reviewed real clips from 675 public source videos across 10 social-risk categories to 16,056 generated counterparts conditioned on each clip's own first frame, drawn from four open-source generators (Wan2.2, LTX, OmniWeaving) and five commercial ones (Kling, Seedance2.0, Runway, Hailuo, HappyHorse). Seven traditional detectors including CNNSpot, UnivFD, DeCoF and ReStraV fall from published AUCs of 67.6 to 98.6 percent down to source-level means of 43.9 to 57.3 percent, and their rankings reshuffle from one generator to the next. We look at RA-Bench-HumanProof, the 633 clips that all five human reviewers judged real, where those same detectors average 47.5 percent AUC, worse than chance, so the videos that fool people are also the ones machines miss. We look at RA-Bench-LastMile, where ordinary sharing operations, a transcode, a downsample, a drop to 8 fps and a synthetic news badge, cut fake recall from 46.0 percent to 1.4 percent. Against that we set Sheng-Yu Wang and colleagues' 2020 finding that a classifier trained on a single generator spots eleven others, a title that hedged with "for now" and is now being cashed; Utkarsh Ojha's CLIP-feature approach to universal detection, which lands at 39.4 percent AUC here; Stable Video Diffusion, the latent-video-diffusion recipe the generators descend from; Matthew Groh and colleagues' study of 15,016 people showing that machine-informed crowds beat either humans or models alone, a remedy that only works while their errors stay uncorrelated; and Deepfake-Eval-2024, which reaches the same verdict from 45 hours of deepfakes actually circulating across 88 websites. We close on what follows if post-hoc detection cannot survive being reshared, and on the harm running the other way, since 22.8 percent of genuine crisis videos in this study were judged fake.
