The Race between Education and Technology (March 2007)

In this episode we unpack Claudia Goldin and Lawrence Katz's NBER working paper, the chapter that gave their book its title and the framework most economists still use to explain what a college degree is worth. The argument: technology has pushed up the demand for educated workers steadily for a century, and whether the college wage premium rises or falls depends on whether schooling keeps up, which it did until about 1980 and then stopped doing.

We walk through their supply, demand and institutions framework, the college wage premium from 1915 to 2005 that collapsed through the 1940s and was back at its 1915 level by 2005, the estimate that a 10 percent rise in the relative supply of college workers cuts the premium by about 6 percent, and the counterfactual that the premium would have fallen after 1980 had college supply kept growing at its 1960 to 1980 pace. We test it with the Katz and Murphy supply and demand analysis it built on, Autor, Goldin and Katz's 2020 extension to 2017 and its puzzling slowdown in demand growth since the early 1990s, Deming and Noray's evidence that the early pay advantage of computer science, engineering and business majors fades as job skills change, and Fairlie and Wu's 2026 finding that unemployment among recent graduates did not spike in summer 2026. We close on what the race means when AI shifts demand, and what universities should do about supply.
