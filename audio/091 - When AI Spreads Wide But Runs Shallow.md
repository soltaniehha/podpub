What Work Does Generative AI Do? (August 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new working paper from Alexander Bick of the St. Louis Fed, Adam Blandin and Tyler Schumacher of Vanderbilt, and David Deming of Harvard, circulated through NBER and the St. Louis Fed. Instead of predicting which jobs AI could touch, they ask a nationally representative sample of American workers which of their own tasks they actually use generative AI for. The answer is that adoption is wide but shallow: generative AI reaches most occupations, yet within most of them fewer than half of workers use it.

We walk through the Real-Time Population Survey and its task module, fielded in four waves from August 2025 to May 2026, which shows each worker the ten most important O*NET work activities for their occupation and builds the first task-level adoption indexes, with data analysis, report writing and modeling at the top and patient care, driving and equipment work at the bottom. We cover how much of real use the standard exposure scores from Eloundou, Felten, Webb and Brynjolfsson actually predict (between 5 and 53 percent of the variation across occupations), why clerical work is more exposed than it is used, and why the chat-log task measures built from Claude, ChatGPT and Copilot conversations line up so weakly with the survey, drawing on the authors' earlier The Rapid Adoption of Generative AI, Tomlinson and colleagues' Working with AI, Handa and colleagues' study of Claude conversations and Eloundou and colleagues' GPTs are GPTs. We close on the paper's argument that who adopts may matter as much as which tasks AI can do, and what that means for how workers learn to use it.
