Why Better Models Can Create Riskier Systems: Evidence from LLM Agents in Financial Markets (September 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new preprint from MIT by Jillian Ross and Zoe De Simone of the EECS department, Eric So and Andrew W. Lo of MIT Sloan, and Charles Pozniak of Harvard Kennedy School's Belfer Center, posted on 3 September 2026. Their claim is one that no benchmark is built to catch: making an individual model better can make the system it is deployed into worse. They call it the capability paradox. The argument runs through correlation. Split what an agent does into a corrective part, the pressure that pushes a system toward the truth, and a non-corrective residual, everything else it brings along. Independent residuals cancel out as you add agents. Correlated ones do not, and they leave a risk floor that no amount of scaling can diversify away.

We walk through the evidence. Across seven frontier models and 21 model pairs, capability significantly predicts correlated non-corrective behaviour on both ELO and MMLU-Pro, while sharing a provider does not, so two capable models from different companies are more alike than two weak ones from the same company. In an agent-based single-asset market with Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Sonnet 4.6 and Gemini 2.0 Flash Lite trading against noise traders, more LLM participation improves price discovery when the shared signal is accurate, and a single parameter, how aggressively each model trades on perceived mispricing, explains the ranking between them. Then the authors feed every agent the same misleading commentary, and the benefit does not merely shrink, it inverts: tracking error climbs to five to seven times the noise-only baseline, and Sonnet's directional accuracy falls to 42 percent, which is below chance. A market of uninformed noise traders beats a market of capable models reading the same wrong story. We set this against Kleinberg and Raghavan's monoculture theorem, Kim and colleagues' measurement of correlated errors across 350 models, and the contested question of why capable models converge at all, where the Platonic Representation Hypothesis and the 2026 paper disputing it disagree in public. We close on the uncomfortable implication: the natural fix, buying from more vendors, does not address the driver, and the evidence here is a simulated market, which the authors say plainly.
