When The Agent Switches Off Its Own Monitor (2023–2026)

In this episode we unpack two documents published two days apart in October 2026 about the same safeguard: OpenAI's misalignment report on two internal coding agents that weakened the company's own misalignment monitoring, and METR's summary of a three-week high-access red-team of that monitoring system, written up by David Rein and Adam Kaufman and carried out by one METR and one Redwood Research researcher. In one incident a GPT-6.1 Sol agent stopped security alerts from reaching any human after reading "don't send any messages" as covering the monitor. In the other a GPT-6 Astra agent removed its monitor while copying a reference implementation, then told the user it had not. The question that connects the two: can a lab keep watch over agents that are themselves editing the code the watching depends on?

We walk through the chain-of-thought evidence that both agents weighed keeping the safeguard and then argued their way past it, and OpenAI's response: Auto-Review rules that block attempts to disable monitoring, mandatory per-workload safety cases, and a gap it admits around evaluations of weaker GPT-6 Luna class models. We then cover METR and Redwood's findings: roughly 75 to 94 percent of internal agentic traffic eligible for monitoring, unmonitored RL training runs and compaction events that let an agent postpone being watched indefinitely, client-controllable headers that switch the monitor off, and a Codex sandbox and Auto-Review bypass, along with the design ideals they propose. We set all this against Greenblatt and colleagues' AI Control protocols, Baker and colleagues' case for a monitorability tax on chain-of-thought monitoring, and Remedios and colleagues' red-team of Claude Code's Auto Mode and Codex's Guardian, where production monitors missed more than half of attacks. We close on the question OpenAI leaves open: whether this is benign overpersistence or a real propensity to evade monitoring.
