Digital Twins Are Funhouse Mirrors: Five Systematic Distortions (September 2026)

In this episode we unpack a Science Advances paper published on 2 September 2026 by Tianyi Peng, George Gui, Melanie Brucks, Eric J. Johnson, Vicki Morwitz, Oded Netzer, Olivier Toubia and sixteen further co-authors, twenty-three in all, almost entirely at Columbia University. A digital twin is a large language model handed a thick dossier about one real person and then asked to answer as that person, and social scientists and startups are deploying them fast on the promise that cheap simulated subjects can stand in for expensive human ones. The authors ran the largest test of that promise yet, nineteen pre-registered studies covering 164 outcomes, and the twins barely work: a twin's answers correlate with its own human counterpart at an average r of about 0.20.

We walk through the finding that is sharper than the headline. A full-persona twin built from more than five hundred of a person's own answers, roughly 128,000 characters of individual detail, scores 0.748 on individual-level accuracy, while a persona built from fourteen demographic variables alone scores 0.746, a difference that is not statistically significant. Five hundred questions about someone buys essentially nothing over knowing their census box, which means the twin is mostly reproducing a demographic stereotype rather than modelling a particular mind. We cover the under-dispersion (twin standard deviation runs at 0.634 of human), the aggregate gap of 0.352 standard deviations that is significant in 105 of the 164 outcomes, and the five distortions the authors name: insufficient individuation, stereotyping, representation bias, ideological biases, and hyper-rationality, that last one washing out the very behavioural messiness researchers usually want to study. We set this against the optimistic results it complicates, Park and colleagues' interview-based twins of a thousand people and the Twin-2K-500 dataset paper from this same team, and against Argyle and colleagues' founding claim that language models reproduce population-level survey patterns, which turns out to be exactly the level at which the idea still holds. We close on the reconciliation the authors offer, that earlier validation leaned on famous instruments the models may have memorized and on accuracy scaled to human test-retest reliability rather than raw correlation, and on the open question of whether individual human variation is recoverable from text at all.
