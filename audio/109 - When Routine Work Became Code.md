The Skill Content of Recent Technological Change: An Empirical Exploration (November 2003)

In this episode we unpack the classic that taught economists to see a job as a bundle of tasks: the Quarterly Journal of Economics paper by David Autor, Frank Levy and Richard Murnane of MIT and Harvard. The argument has organized the debate for two decades. Computers carry out rules-based procedures, so they substitute for routine work that can be written down step by step and complement the non-routine problem solving and communication that cannot, which means technology reshapes what people do inside their jobs rather than simply deleting jobs.

We walk through the routine versus non-routine, cognitive versus manual task framework, the falling price of computing as the causal force, and the Dictionary of Occupational Titles measures the authors use to track task input across the US workforce from 1960 to 1998, including the finding that much of the shift happened within occupations rather than between them. We then test how the idea held up: Autor and Dorn's 2013 account of job polarization and low-skill service growth, Atalay and coauthors' job-ad evidence that task change inside job titles was even larger than first measured, Autor's 2015 "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs?", Eloundou and coauthors' finding that large language models reach the higher-wage cognitive work the framework treated as shielded, and Chen and coauthors' 2026 reading of 750 million job ads, in which tasks outlive the jobs that carried them. We close on what a task view means for what universities teach and credential when the line between routine and non-routine is itself moving.
