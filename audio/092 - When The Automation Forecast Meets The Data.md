Rethinking Automation Risk: AI Applicability and Occupational Outcomes, 2019–24 (July 2026)

In this episode we unpack a Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago working paper by Kristen Broady and Caleb Dunson of the Chicago Fed with Anthony Barr of the National Bankers Association, which goes back to the most famous jobs-at-risk forecast of the last decade and checks it against what happened. They set Frey and Osborne's 2017 automation probabilities and Microsoft's 2025 AI applicability scores against US employment and wage data for 2019 to 2024, and conclude that exposure scores are better read as signs that an occupation will be restructured than as forecasts that it will disappear.

We walk through the paper's revisit of Frey and Osborne's three engineering bottlenecks (perception and manipulation, creative intelligence, social intelligence) in light of generative AI and modern robotics, and its comparison of 70 matched occupations, where high-risk jobs lost about 3 percent of employment, moderate-risk jobs about 6 percent, low-risk jobs grew, and wages rose in every group, with telemarketers falling by half and models more than doubling inside the same high-risk category. We set it against Frey and Osborne's original forecast that 47 percent of US jobs were at high risk, Tomlinson and colleagues' Copilot-based applicability scores, Eloundou and colleagues' language-model exposure measure, and Hernæs and Kostøl's tracking of all private-sector employment in Norway, which finds almost no gap between the most and least exposed occupations since ChatGPT. We close on what the evidence does and does not show about forecasting, and on why the strongest claims about early-career workers still need better data.
