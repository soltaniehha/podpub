Science at the Frontier: How Scientists Use ChatGPT (November 2026) + Large Language Models Have Not Yet Transformed Science (November 2026)

In this episode we unpack two new studies released a day apart that ask the same question from opposite ends: is AI changing how science gets done? From inside the chat window, Chris Liao, Carl Shan and Aaron Chatterji of OpenAI's Economic Research team analyze 580,186 scientific messages from 1,819 academic scientists in the ChatGPT for Academic Researchers program and survey 251 of them, and nearly all respondents expect AI to speed up discovery in their field. From the published record, Ron Bekkerman, Nichole Li, Bahareh Kargar and Mark Somers of the New Jersey Institute of Technology trace 2.5 million full-text papers across five disciplines from 2015 to 2025 and find that, apart from some word choices, most trends that were already under way simply continued after 2022.

We walk through OpenAI's taxonomy of 10 scientific workflows and 40 tasks, the finding that reading, writing, scientific analysis and research software make up 85 percent of the average scientist's scientific messages, the split between AI-proposed and scientist-supplied research questions, the follow-ups scientists send on about a third of delegated tasks, and the self-reported productivity ratios that run from 1.35x in administration to 2.83x in coding, alongside the bottlenecks of time, staffing and funding that remain. We then turn to Bekkerman and colleagues' segmented regressions over 14,484 word lemmas (796 surge words, with "delve" rising and then falling), lexical diversity and scientific-terminology density, topic diversity measured by Jensen-Shannon distance, the boilerplate artifact behind an apparent biomedical narrowing, and the one real break, faster growth in Asian computer science coauthor teams. We set both against Hao, Xu, Li and Evans's Nature finding that AI expands individual scientists' impact while contracting science's focus, Kusumegi and coauthors' evidence of an LLM-driven surge in paper production, and Liang and coauthors' measurement of LLM-modified text, and close on why individual speedups and an unchanged scientific record can both be true.
