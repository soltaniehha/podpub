When The Machines Come For The Middle (2002-2026)

In this episode we unpack "Automation and Optimal Taxation: A Task-Based Theory", an NBER working paper released on 14 September 2026 by Henrik Kleven and Owen Zidar of Princeton. The policy debate about automation has focused on taxing the robots themselves. This paper argues that the bigger lever is the ordinary income tax: automation leaves the classic optimal tax formulas intact, but it changes the rates they prescribe substantially, calling for a much more generous earned income tax credit at the bottom, higher taxes on workers machines cannot replace, and a sizable tax on capital income.

We walk through the task-based model, in which 1,000 occupations are automatable from the bottom of the skill distribution up to a threshold and machines pin down wages in the jobs they can do, and the compensated perturbation approach that shows the Saez (2002) participation tax formula and the Saez-Stantcheva capital tax rule survive in full general equilibrium through the targeting logic of Diamond and Mirrlees. We cover the US calibration, where the optimal work subsidy reaches 77 percent of earnings against 36 percent without automation, taxes above the threshold run about 38 to 47 percent, machine intensity peaks around the 41st wage percentile so that the middle class rather than the lowest paid takes the largest hit, and the optimal capital tax sits at 50 percent regardless of automation. We set it against Saez's case for work subsidies, Acemoglu and Restrepo's evidence that automation drove 50 to 70 percent of US wage structure changes, the transitional and small robot taxes of Guerreiro, Rebelo and Teles and of Costinot and Werning, and Acemoglu, Manera and Restrepo's argument that the US tax code favors automation, and close on the model's no-retraining assumption, what changes if AI automates high-skill tasks first, and whether cheaper machines call for a robot tax or a bigger EITC.
