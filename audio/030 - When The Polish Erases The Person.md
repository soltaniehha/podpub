The Shrinking Landscape of Linguistic Diversity in the Age of Large Language Models (August 2026)

In this episode we unpack a study published in Nature Human Behaviour on 24 August 2026 by Zhivar Sourati, Farzan Karimi-Malekabadi, Meltem Ozcan, Colin McDaniel, Alireza Ziabari, Jackson Trager, Ala N. Tak, Meng Chen, Fred Morstatter and Morteza Dehghani, working across computer science, psychology and the Information Sciences Institute at the University of Southern California. Their claim is that language is not just a way to move information around: it is a fingerprint, carrying signals about who wrote it, how old they are, what they believe and how they feel. Across three studies, seven datasets and more than 880,000 texts, they find that as large language models spread into everyday writing, that fingerprint is being sanded down. The meaning survives the edit. The person, to a measurable degree, does not.

We walk through the evidence in the order they build it. First the observational half: monthly writing-complexity variance in 318,490 Reddit stories, 379,583 Patch News articles and 80,238 arXiv abstracts, with AI-written text flagged by the Binoculars detector, run through discontinuous growth models and Granger causality tests that locate a sharp drop at ChatGPT's release and a sustained decline afterwards. Then the controlled half, where a thousand pre-ChatGPT human texts are handed to GPT-3.5, Llama 3 70B and Gemini Pro under a dozen neutral prompts like clarity, polish and improve readability: semantic similarity stays above 0.95 for 87 percent of texts while the variance in writing complexity falls by 21 to 50 percent. The consequence lands in Study 2, where classifiers trained to read a person off their prose lose ground on every trait tested, worst on age, and shift in a consistent direction, reading rewritten authors as older, more male, more politically liberal, more moralistic and less empathetic than they actually are. Study 3 shows why: the erosion is selective rather than random, with the link between gender and negative-emotion words effectively erased while neuroticism's signal survives intact. We set this beside Padmakumar and He on co-writing, Doshi and Hauser's finding that generative AI makes individual stories better and the collective narrower, Agarwal and colleagues on suggestions that pull writing toward Western norms, Wang and colleagues on models flattening identity groups, and Kosinski's foundational demonstration that digital traces predict private traits at all, and close on what breaks downstream: mental-health screening that reads distress in word choice, hiring that rewards conventional polish, and a sociolinguistics whose instruments assume nobody ran the text through a model first.
