Autonomous AI Agents Discover Reverse Transcriptases with Tandem Repeat Arrays (September 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new preprint from Anthropic's life sciences lab, written by Peter Yoon, Januka Athukoralage, Emmanuel Ameisen, Eric Kauderer-Abrams, Nicholas Perry and Matthew Durrant and released on 23 September 2026 alongside the announcement of the lab itself. The argument: a general-purpose language model, left to run a genome-mining campaign on its own, can do the part of discovery that has always needed a human expert, which is looking at raw data and noticing that something is odd.

We walk through the agentic harness of worker and supervisor Claude agents that surveyed reverse transcriptases across 1.9 billion metagenomic protein clusters (119 tasks, 949 agent sessions, 21.5 hours, no human intervention), the moment a worker read raw DNA beside a jumbo-phage enzyme and flagged a CRISPR-like tandem repeat array, and the new ART family that came out of it, with its RNA arrays among the most abundant transcripts during Staphylococcus phage SA1 infection. We cover the ten reruns that all missed the array, the fixed-input benchmark separating Opus 5.5, Mythos 5.1, Mythos 5 and Opus 5 from older models, the finding that more tools made models less likely to see the repeats, and the Mythos 5 internal repeat signals compared with the genome language models Evo 2 and gLM2. We set it against the Arc Institute's Minerva, which found similar RNA arrays beside UG27 reverse transcriptases the same week, the Weizmann retron work it builds on, the Kosmos AI scientist and Kitano's Nobel Turing Challenge, and close on what is still unproven: nobody yet knows what ART does, and one lucky run out of eleven is a discovery, not a method.
