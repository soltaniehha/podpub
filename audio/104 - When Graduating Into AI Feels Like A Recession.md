Graduating into Disruption: Labor Market Outcomes for AI-Exposed College Majors (September 2026)

In this episode we unpack a U.S. Census Bureau working paper by Cody Orr, Lee Tucker and Lawrence Warren that follows 6.7 million bachelor's graduates from more than 350 institutions, CUNY among them, into their first jobs. The argument: since ChatGPT arrived, graduates of the majors most exposed to AI, led by computer science and its neighbors, have been about 5 percentage points less likely to be employed the quarter after graduation and have earned about 13 percent less when they were, a hit as large as graduating into a big recession.

We walk through how the authors build AI exposure for each major from Eloundou and colleagues' GPT-4 task scores and the jobs young graduates actually hold, the event-study design comparing majors within the same college, and the finding that about half the earnings loss comes from graduates landing in retail and restaurants instead of information and professional services. We test it against Tucker's companion study of early-career hiring by industry, Oreopoulos, von Wachter and Heisz's classic evidence on graduating in a recession, Lambert and Schindler's argument that remote work rather than AI explains the broken entry ladder, and Chirikov's finding that A grades rose 13 points in AI-exposed courses, which may be blurring the signal a degree sends. We close on what universities can do when the first rung of the career ladder moves.
