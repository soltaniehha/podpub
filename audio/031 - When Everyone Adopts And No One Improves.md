Sophistication in GenAI Use: Field Evidence from a Large Firm (August 2026)

In this episode we unpack a working paper posted on 27 August 2026 by Nicholas J. Hallman, Zachary T. Kowaleski and Jaime J. Schmidt of the University of Texas at Austin, with Anu Puvvada of KPMG LLP, who got hold of something researchers almost never see: the actual conversation transcripts. Inside KPMG's back office they observe 713,564 employee prompts across 158,496 conversations from nearly 4,000 employees in 15 functional areas over eight months of 2025. Adoption is not the question here, because it is already effectively settled, with 83.9 percent of employee-months including genAI use against the 32.1 percent a national survey found the same year. The question is whether anyone is actually good at it. Their answer is that skill tracks rank rather than youth, that it barely moves across eight months, and that the firm's formal AI training shows up in the month an employee takes it and then disappears.

We walk through how the measure was built, since that is half the contribution: every transcript is scored by an LLM into use cases and three composites, prompt clarity, deliberate strategy use and use-case diversity. What follows is unusually concrete. Writing is 73.3 percent of all conversations. The entire apparatus of prompt engineering, chain-of-thought, few-shot examples, self-checking, interactive refinement, appears in 5.2 percent of conversations, each individual technique in under 1 percent, while 30.2 percent contain pleasantries and 10.7 percent contain typos. Sophistication rises monotonically with seniority, staff below managers below the ranks above them, and swings hard across functions: Project Management, Strategy and Digital Innovation lead, while Accounting and Finance trails on all three measures inside an accounting firm. We set this against Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond's call-center experiment, where a generative assistant helped the least experienced agents most and the best ones slightly less than not at all; Humlum and Vestergaard's Danish survey, where younger workers adopt more and every year of experience predicts less use; Dillon and colleagues' randomized Copilot trial across 56 firms, where the gains landed only on the roughly 40 percent who used it regularly; Handa and colleagues' map of four million Claude conversations; and Schulhoff's catalogue of 58 prompting techniques that the shop floor has essentially never encountered. We close on the finding that should worry anyone running a rollout: longer opening prompts and more iteration predict skilled use, sheer frequency does not, and the training did not stick.
