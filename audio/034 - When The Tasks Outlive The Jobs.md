When The Tasks Outlive The Jobs (2003-2026)

In this episode we unpack a new working paper from Qin Chen of Shanghai DeepPulse Technology, Ying Fang and Xiangyu Wang of the Wang Yanan Institute for Studies in Economics at Xiamen University, and Leo Yang Yang of Hong Kong Baptist University, posted to arXiv on 27 August 2026. They read 752.6 million Chinese job ads posted between January 2022 and June 2026 and ask a question the usual instruments cannot answer: when the work most exposed to language models appears to be vanishing, is it the tasks that are disappearing, or only the job titles that used to carry them? Measuring both layers in the same data, they find the second. We pair it with four earlier papers that built the task approach to labor economics and the exposure measures everyone now uses, and one very recent one that reports the payroll evidence this new result reinterprets.

We walk through the instrument itself, a dynamic O*NET for China built from employer language rather than questionnaires: 20,721 requirements a candidate must meet and 44,479 tasks a hire will do, 65,200 catalog items matched back to 12.1 billion item-posting mentions, sliceable by city, wage band and month, with expensive model judgment spent only on reusable artifacts so that adjudicating 520,000 vocabulary boundaries cost about 3,300 yuan instead of the 9.7 million a per-document approach would have. The headline is a two-layer comparison: occupations more exposed to language models lose posting share at a slope of -1.33, while the task categories inside them lose mention share at only -0.83, and both a shift-share and a three-component Shapley decomposition put that entire gradient in the posting-share channel rather than in any stripping of exposed content from surviving jobs. We spend time on the two worked examples, the accountant whose bookkeeping core falls from 68% to 49% of task mentions across wage bands while the credential gate moves from the junior certificate to the intermediate one, and the production worker whose LLM exposure runs from under 0.2 to 0.48 within a single title, against the Java developer who carries the same bundle at every level and for whom the occupational average is simply fine. Autor, Levy and Murnane gave us the task framework and the finding that change within identical occupations explains most of computerization's demand shift; Atalay and colleagues found the same within-title pattern across a century of newspaper ads; Frey and Osborne supplied the 47%-at-risk occupation-level estimate this paper argues collapses an occupation to a point; Eloundou and colleagues moved exposure from occupations to tasks; and Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen report US payroll data in which employment of 22-to-25-year-olds in AI-exposed occupations sits 19% below trend. We close on the disagreement the authors leave open, that the within-occupation channel two decades of research called the main event reads as roughly flat over these 32 months, and on what it means for policy that occupation-level exposure scores are least reliable exactly where attention concentrates.
