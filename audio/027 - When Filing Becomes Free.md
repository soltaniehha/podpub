When Filing Becomes Free (2019–2026)

In this episode we unpack a new paper from Chris Schmitz of the Hertie School's Centre for Digital Governance, Lewis Hammond of the Cooperative AI Foundation, and Alan Chan of GovAI, posted to arXiv on 17 August 2026 and forthcoming at the AAAI Conference on AI, Ethics and Society. They name a phenomenon they call agentic flooding: a surge in the volume or complexity of requests hitting a government service, caused by people using AI, and big enough to strain the service. Their evidence is a dataset of 84 cases across 11 jurisdictions and 13 service domains, screened from roughly 2,288 candidate services, where in 69 percent of cases government officials themselves are the ones attributing the surge to AI. We read it alongside four older sources that supply the theory it stands on, and the tension that makes it interesting: friction in public bureaucracy is both a barrier to access and, in the older economic literature, a deliberate rationing device.

We walk through the cases that make it concrete, from Australian freedom of information requests to German social courts attributing a 55 percent jump in 2025 caseload to AI-generated claims, environmental comment letters running past 4,000 pages, and organized voter roll purge submissions in the United States. We cover the paper's split between quantitative and qualitative flooding, its finding that 87 percent of cases run on nothing more exotic than cheap legally sophisticated text with a human still driving the process, and its 13-factor risk matrix pointing at tax administration and court systems as the most exposed. Halling and Baekgaard's systematic review of 119 studies gives us the administrative burden framework of learning, compliance and psychological costs that the paper maps agent capabilities onto. Zeckhauser's work on ordeals gives us the uncomfortable counterpoint, that burdens which help nobody are chosen precisely to sort scarce subsidized resources toward those who value them most, which reframes agentic flooding as what happens when AI removes the ordeal without replacing the screen. Sharp, Bilgin, Gabriel and Hammond on agentic inequality explains why the fastest government response, adding friction back, falls hardest on poorer and less digitally literate users, and Piedrahita and colleagues, writing with Bengio, Russell and Schölkopf, arrive at the same institutional overload from pure theory. We close on the trade-off the authors leave open: suppress demand and you ration access, build capacity and you need years you may not have.
