The Economics of Recursive Self-Improvement (September 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new working paper from Tom Cunningham of METR and eight coauthors at the Elasticity Institute, spanning Stanford, MIT, Columbia, Carnegie Mellon, Yale, the University of Virginia and Epoch AI. Their subject is the most hyped question in AI: whether AI systems improving AI research will tip into a self-sustaining acceleration. Their contribution is to stop arguing about it and attach a number to it, and the number says the loop is not closed yet, though it is tightening.

We walk through their graphical framework, where each model is a directed graph whose edges are elasticities and where self-sustaining acceleration arrives exactly when the total elasticity of algorithmic improvement with respect to the stock of algorithms passes one. We build up from the Jones (1995) innovation model to the core feedback loop between algorithms and capabilities, then the bottlenecks (human labor, experimental compute, inference compute, training compute, data), the distinction between narrow capabilities that feed back into AI R&D and broad capabilities that do not, and the economic loops where revenue buys the next round of compute. Then the calibration: measured on the Epoch Capabilities Index, the condition is met if one ECI unit buys 15 percent higher AI R&D productivity, and their estimate of the real return is about 9 percent. We sit with the weak link in that estimate, since it descends from Anthropic engineers self-reporting a 4X uplift while Becker, Rush, Barnes and Rein's METR randomized trial found experienced developers were measurably slower with AI and thought they were faster, and we bring in Manso, Fu and Thompson's 2,507 head-to-head comparisons across 27 disciplines, where AI is both costlier and worse in nearly a quarter of cases. We close on the bottlenecks, the political barriers, and the authors' unglamorous ask: the measurements AI companies could publish to settle this.
