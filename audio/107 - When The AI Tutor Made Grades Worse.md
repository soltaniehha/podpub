The Effects of Course-Integrated AI Tutoring on Student Performance and Engagement: A Randomized University Trial (October 2026)

In this episode we unpack a randomized trial from Jing Liu and colleagues at the University of Maryland, run to decide whether to put an AI tutor in every course on campus. Thirty instructors teaching 2,379 undergraduates across disciplines were randomized so that whole sections got a GPT-4o study assistant built into the course website or did not, and the result surprised the researchers: within sections of the same course, access lowered final grades by 0.37 standard deviations, with the largest losses among first-generation students.

We walk through the design, the sharp drop in engagement with the course site (participation down 0.90 standard deviations), the fact that only about 15 percent of students ever used the tool and non-users lost ground too, and the authors' explanation that the tool changed the classroom's norms about AI. Then we set it against four other 2026 experiments: Gallegos finds that encouraging students to adopt a course AI assistant does not improve scores while guidance on using it to learn raises exam scores by 0.21 standard deviations, Oreopoulos and Low find Khanmigo's coaching tutor barely used in a two-year Tennessee trial, Oreopoulos, Liut, Sungu and Low find the most encouraging learning gains when AI is built into a mastery workflow, and Brosch and colleagues find AI lets German IT apprentices perform tasks beyond their training. We close on why AI can raise performance on the job while lowering learning in the classroom, and what universities should do before switching on AI in every course.
