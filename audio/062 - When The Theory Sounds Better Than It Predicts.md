When The Theory Sounds Better Than It Predicts (2024–2026)

In this episode we unpack "Artificial intelligences and human scientists exhibit complementary strengths in theory building", an 80 page preprint posted to arXiv on 26 September 2026 by Ke Li, Spyros Zoumpoulis, Phanish Puranam, Philip Parker and Eric Uhlmann of INSEAD with more than 80 co-authors, among them Igor Grossmann and Robb Willer. The study pits 25 large language models against 13 senior social scientists and 60 doctoral students at the creative core of research: building a theory, predicting what the data will show, and revising after the data comes in. The models beat most individual humans, but human scientists were far more diverse, formed wiser crowds, and got more predictive accuracy out of simpler theories.

We walk through the design, which asks every theorist to anticipate what predicts racial and gender inequality discourse across roughly 175,000 papers from Semantic Scholar, scored against a random forest benchmark, and the results: AI tripling human accuracy on racial inequality interactions, the aggregated human crowd winning on main effects because its predictions were 86 percent more diverse, and AI theories with more causal paths and latent variables that blind human and GPT-5.5 raters preferred even though the extra complexity did not predict better. We cover the finding that the models revised their theories 42 percent more than humans but without regard to how wrong they had been, and we set the paper against Ashokkumar, Hewitt, Ghezae and Willer's work on LLMs predicting social science experiments, the AUTOCOG automated cognitive scientist, Doshi and Hauser's evidence that AI raises individual creativity while shrinking collective diversity, and the Artificial Hivemind claim of cross-model homogeneity, alongside a Stanford reanalysis published the next day arguing that the Hivemind evidence does not hold up. We close on the "one AI, many humans" team, whether science risks drifting into a monoculture, and why theories that impress reviewers are not the same as theories that predict.
