What Work Can Robots Do? (September 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new study from Anthropic's economics team, Russell Legate-Yang and Maxim Massenkoff, that asks a question the AI-and-jobs debate has mostly skipped: what can physical robots actually do today, and at what price? Their answer is that robots can already perform about three quarters of the physical tasks in the US economy, a third of all working hours, but are cheaper than the people doing that work for just 0.3 percent of tasks.

We walk through their robot exposure index, built by having Claude search for real deployed robots across 7,594 physical O*NET tasks and rate each on a four-tier environmental-control rubric (E0 to E3), why taxi drivers top the index while nurses and mechanics barely register, how packers and packagers became the largest job where robots already win on cost, and the 1977 backtest linking exposure to later wage and employment declines. We set it against Frey and Osborne's 47 percent forecast, Autor's argument about why there are still so many jobs, Acemoglu and Restrepo's evidence from industrial robots, and the LLM exposure measures of Eloundou et al. and Massenkoff and McCrory, and close on the 40-year cost runway, whether an LLM can be trusted to judge robots, and why AI's cognitive and physical waves land on almost opposite groups of workers.
