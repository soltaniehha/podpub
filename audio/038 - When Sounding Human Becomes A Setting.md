Perceived Humanness And Empathy Are Dissociated In Relationship Advice Generated By A Large Language Model (September 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new Nature Communications paper from Bennett Kleinberg and six colleagues at Tilburg University, University College London, the University of Amsterdam, and the IMT School of Advanced Studies in Lucca, published on 3 September 2026 and circulated earlier as the preprint "Linguistic traces of stochastic empathy in language models". Across five experiments they ask something most AI-detection work skips: what happens when you tell both sides they are in a contest? When people and GPT-4 are each instructed to sound as human as possible, only the model can comply. Human writing barely moves, the model's perceived humanness jumps, and the gap that lets readers tell the two apart very nearly closes.

We walk through the design, which uses relationship advice precisely because it demands real perspective taking, and compares naive, adversarial, and evasion instructions with thousands of Prolific participants rating each text on a five-point source scale. The numbers carry the argument: a human advantage of d=0.82 that holds across every study, a swing of roughly 39 percent in the model's perceived humanness against 2.6 percent for people, and readers' diagnostic accuracy falling from an AUC of 0.83 to 0.62 once the model is told to try. We spend time on Study 4, where instructing GPT-4o to be empathic makes its writing read as less human even as raters judge it more empathic on all three dimensions, which breaks the obvious explanation and earns the paper its central idea of stochastic empathy, empathic language produced with no access to what empathy is for. Study 5 exposes the mechanism as pure style, with "dear friend" giving way to "hey", netspeak and self-reference climbing, and vocabulary getting simpler. We set all of this against Jakesch, Hancock and Naaman on why human heuristics for spotting AI are flawed, Yin, Jia and Wakslak on how an AI label destroys the feeling of being heard, Osborne and Bailey on how people judge machine advice, Rahwan and colleagues' machine behaviour framework, and Bender and colleagues' stochastic parrots, and close on what happens to trust in online writing when sounding human is a setting only one side can switch on.
