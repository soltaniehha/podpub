When Scaling Laws Reach The Body (2020–2026)

In this episode we unpack SONIC, published in Science Robotics on 12 August 2026 by Zhengyi Luo, Ye Yuan, Tingwu Wang and twenty-five colleagues at NVIDIA, with Linxi "Jim" Fan and Yuke Zhu advising. The claim is that the recipe that built large language models finally works on a physical body. Humanoid controllers have stayed stubbornly small, often three-layer networks trained on a handful of GPUs for one task at a time, each new skill demanding its own hand-engineered reward. SONIC's argument is that the obstacle was never the hardware, it was the choice of training task: pick motion tracking, which takes dense frame-by-frame supervision straight from motion capture and needs no reward engineering at all, and the familiar curves come back. Performance rises steadily with model size, data and compute, and it rises most on motions the robot has never seen, which is to say that scale buys generalization rather than just fit.

We walk through the three scaling axes and what each one buys: networks from 1.2 million to 42 million parameters, data from 4 million to 100 million frames drawn from roughly 700 hours and 317,000 clips of motion capture, and compute from 2,000 to 21,000 GPU hours, 128 GPUs running for seven days at the top end, trained in Isaac Lab and deployed on a Unitree G1. The largest model reaches 99.6 percent success at 23.8mm mean per-joint error on held-out motion content, against 98.0 percent and 27.7mm for the smallest. Against rival trackers it takes 98.5, 99.2 and 97.2 percent on test-content, test-repetition and PHUMA, where BeyondMimic manages 82.0, 85.4 and 73.8 and Any2Track 61.6, 69.4 and 78.5, cutting tracking error 42 percent below BeyondMimic. The sharper result is against a specialist: OpenHomie, the open release of Ben and colleagues' HOMIE system, is a single-task locomotion controller built for exactly this job, and it survives 43.0 percent of runs where the generalist SONIC survives 98.5, holding near-total stability to about 4 metres per second where the specialist collapses past 2. On the physical robot, 124 motions transfer at 99.2 percent, with the feet the weak point at 53.7mm against 29.0 in simulation. We set this against Kaplan and colleagues' 2020 power laws, the source of the borrowed recipe, and ask whether three points on a log axis really earn the name; against Peng's AMP and Tessler's MaskedMimic, the adversarial and masked-inpainting lineage that tried to unify character control and, SONIC argues, runs into mode collapse exactly as the motion dataset grows large enough to scale on; and against the team's own ASAP, which closed the simulation-to-reality dynamics gap all of this depends on. We close on the real-time kinematic planner that replans every 100 milliseconds in under 5 milliseconds on a laptop, the universal token space that lets VR teleoperation, video, text, music and vision-language-action models drive one policy without retraining, and the question underneath the whole result: whether human motion capture is a foundation or a ceiling, since a robot that learns only from recorded human movement inherits only human movement, and motion capture cannot be scraped the way text can.
