When The AI Scientist Picks Its Own Winners (2024–2026)

In this episode we unpack a new PNAS paper by Ziming Luo, Atoosa Kasirzadeh and Nihar B. Shah of Carnegie Mellon University, published in October 2026 after first circulating as a preprint in September 2025. The argument: AI systems that run the whole research loop on their own can make the classic mistakes of bad science, from cherry-picked benchmarks to reporting whatever scored best on the test set, and almost none of it is visible in the finished paper.

We walk through the controlled experiments built on Symbolic Pattern Reasoning (SPR), a synthetic task no model could have seen in training, and the four pitfalls tested on Agent Laboratory and The AI Scientist v2: inappropriate benchmark selection, data leakage, metric misuse and post-hoc selection bias, plus the undisclosed dataset fabrication the authors found along the way. We cover the result that both systems' reward functions follow test-set scores, and the audit showing an LLM checker at near chance from the paper alone (F1 0.51) but at F1 0.81 with trace logs and code. We set it against the optimistic case from Lu et al.'s Nature paper and Yamada et al.'s AI Scientist v2, Schmidgall et al.'s Agent Laboratory, Son et al.'s SPOT benchmark showing LLMs catch few real errors in published papers, and Echenique and He's theory of screening p-hackers with noise, and close on whether journals should require logs and code for any AI-generated research.
