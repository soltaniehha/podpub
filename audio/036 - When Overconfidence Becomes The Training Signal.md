When Overconfidence Becomes The Training Signal (2015-2026)

In this episode we unpack a Nature Machine Intelligence paper published on 28 August 2026 by Bojana Ranković and Philippe Schwaller of EPFL, with Ryan-Rhys Griffiths of QuantumGreen. Every laboratory faces the same expensive question: given a vast space of candidates and a limited budget, which experiment do we run next? Bayesian optimization answers it well but leans on hand-engineered representations that rarely transfer between chemistry and materials science. Large language models carry broad scientific knowledge and take instructions in plain English, but they hallucinate and have no calibrated sense of their own uncertainty, which is disqualifying when a wrong guess costs a week of bench time. The paper's move is to stop treating that overconfidence as the flaw that rules language models out, and start using it as the signal that trains them. We pair the result with the paper that supplies its machinery and four recent papers that reach conflicting verdicts on whether language models belong in experimental optimization at all.

We walk through GOLLuM, Gaussian process Optimized LLMs, which trains a language model jointly with a Gaussian process through the GP's marginal likelihood, so gradients from the probabilistic objective reshape the model's own embeddings. Across 23 tasks in organic synthesis, materials science and catalysis, analytic and process chemistry, and molecular design, it ranks first on average, reaching 36.3 percent top-5 percent coverage at a fifty-experiment budget against 29.7 percent for a Gaussian process with domain-tailored features, 26.5 percent for BoChemian and 12.1 percent for LAPEFT, every run cold-started from just ten failed experiments. On the Buchwald-Hartwig cross-coupling benchmark it nearly doubles the discovery of high-yielding conditions, 43 percent against the 24 to 25 percent managed by expert quantum-chemical descriptors and frontier language models, and it matches conventional Bayesian optimization using a median of 41 percent fewer experiments. Two diagnostic findings carry the argument. Static language model embeddings are poor chemical descriptors, recovering only 15 to 26 percent of the best reactions where a specialized reaction fingerprint recovers 38 percent, and what predicts success is not the surrogate's raw accuracy but the geometry of the representation, with the ratio of learned lengthscale to average pairwise distance correlating at 0.92 across fourteen encoders. The Gaussian process objective turns out to act as a contrastive loss nobody wrote down, so by the fiftieth iteration the latent space has sorted itself into a chemically legible map with reactive iodides clustered away from sluggish chlorides, and the model's suggestion can be interrogated before anyone runs it. Andrew Gordon Wilson and colleagues supplied the underlying deep kernel learning recipe back in 2015; Chengrun Yang and colleagues at Google DeepMind argued in OPRO that you can simply prompt a model for the next point, which this paper tests and finds failing between 10 and 80 percent of the time on hallucinated or out-of-bounds proposals; Agustinus Kristiadi and colleagues took a deliberately sober look and concluded language models help only when pre-trained on domain data, a claim undercut here by general-purpose models beating a chemistry-specific one; Tung Nguyen and colleagues held that fixed embeddings are already sufficient, which is precisely the failure mode this paper anatomizes; and Robert MacKnight and colleagues at Carnegie Mellon reported the opposite result a year earlier, that pre-trained knowledge alone lets frontier models match or beat Bayesian optimization on categorical reaction spaces. We close on that unresolved disagreement and on the paper's wider suggestion, that what specializes a foundation model for a high-stakes domain may be richer uncertainty-guided information rather than more data.
