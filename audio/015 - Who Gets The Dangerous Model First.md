Who Gets The Dangerous Model First (2022-2026)

In this episode we unpack a 77 page working paper released through the NBER in August 2026 by Joshua S. Gans of the University of Toronto's Rotman School of Management, on how to regulate an AI model that finds security flaws. The motivating fact is real and very recent: pointed at the world's open source code, an unreleased frontier model surfaced 23,019 findings, 6,202 of them initially rated high or critical, with 90.6 per cent of a sampled subset later validated. The same class of capability had already been used offensively in what its developer called the first largely autonomous cyber espionage campaign. Policy has responded with two instruments that were never designed together: a voluntary thirty day head start for government evaluators under Executive Order 14409, and an unsettled patchwork of liability rules. Gans argues that the two cannot be chosen separately, and that staged access and liability are complements rather than substitutes.

We walk through the flaw contest at the heart of the model, where a defender and an adversary search the same hidden stock of vulnerabilities and every flaw leaves the pool either patched or exploited, and the winner independence property that collapses it into one contest played flaw by flaw. We cover why liability is a weak instrument once a model is public, with a pass through bounded below one half so that doubling the liability rate cuts the exploited share by less than half because the adversary simply searches harder too, and why an exclusive window changes that arithmetic completely: before release, the extra defensive effort liability buys goes uncontested. We get into the deadline effect, where defensive effort climbs through the window and reaches full contest intensity exactly at release by the same logic Mortensen found in job search and benefit exhaustion, and the resulting insight that headway is drawdown, so what matters is protection depth rather than elapsed days. We then set the paper against the work it builds on and argues with: Toby Shevlane's structured access, which named the practice of controlling who may use a model and turned release into a question of access rather than publication; Landolt and colleagues' Oracle's Gambit, which reaches a strikingly similar conclusion from game theory, showing defender welfare turns on the capability gap rather than the shared level, so that handing one model to both sides traps defenders in a Red Queen's race; Koh and Sanguanmoo's adaptive speed limits, the rival timing instrument that caps how fast a technology may scale; and Kapoor, Bommasani, Narayanan and co-authors' marginal risk framework for open foundation models, which pushes back hardest by arguing the evidence for added misuse risk is thinner than the debate assumes. We close on the three findings most likely to unsettle a policymaker: optimal evaluation windows are bounded, so greater danger cannot justify waiting indefinitely; the efficient liability rate is partial rather than full compensation, because defensive search burns real resources; and liability alone cannot make a developer pick the regulator's release date, which is precisely what leaves a distinct job for a timing mandate.
