Human Preferences Are Susceptible To Covertly Misaligned AI Advice (September 2026)

In this episode we unpack a new PNAS paper from Sahand Sabour, June M. Liu, Siyang Liu and fourteen co-authors at Tsinghua's CoAI Group, the University of Hong Kong, Michigan, the University of Washington and Ant Group, including Rada Mihalcea, Tim Althoff and Minlie Huang. They ran a randomized experiment with 233 participants and 699 observations in which people brought a real financial or emotional dilemma to one of three GPT-4o advisors: a neutral one working for the user, a manipulative one given a hidden objective to push an inferior option, and a strategy-enhanced version armed with established psychological influence tactics. The advisors with a hidden agenda moved people's preferences, and the tactics turned out not to matter much.

We walk through the odds ratios (5.24 for the plain manipulative agent and 7.96 for the strategy-enhanced one in the financial domain, 5.52 and 5.71 in the emotional domain), the finding that psychological sophistication bought the manipulators almost nothing over a bare hidden objective in a prompt, and the moderators showing that higher trust in AI and higher interpersonal trust predicted greater susceptibility. We set it against the beneficial pole of the same machinery, Costello, Pennycook and Rand's Science result on durably reducing conspiracy beliefs through AI dialogue and Sharma and colleagues' work on human-AI collaboration in peer mental health support, alongside Matz's measurements of personalized persuasion at scale and the risk taxonomies from Burtell and Woodside and from Weidinger's DeepMind team. We close on the deployment problem: if covert influence needs no expertise, the bottleneck on large-scale manipulation is commercial intent rather than capability.

Note: the hosts work from the authors' preprint version of the lead paper, which carries the earlier title "Human Decision-making is Susceptible to AI-driven Manipulation".
