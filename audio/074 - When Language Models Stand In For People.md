Simulated Responses From Large Language Models Are Weak Proxies For Human Economic Preferences Across 74 Countries (October 2026) + AI-Generated Language Remains Systematically Distinguishable From Human Communication (October 2026)

In this episode we unpack two papers published on the same day that ask whether a language model can stand in for real people in social science research. In Nature Human Behaviour, Fadong Chen, Qijun Wang and Lei Wang of Zhejiang University, with Sudeep Bhatia of the University of Pennsylvania, rerun the Global Preference Survey with machines in place of its human respondents. In PNAS, Nicolò Pagan, Petter Törnberg, Christopher Bail and Christopher Barrie test whether AI-written social media replies can pass for the people they imitate (the published version of a November 2025 preprint). The answer from both directions is the same: the models get the broad strokes right and miss the person.

We walk through the virtual Global Preference Survey, which matches 67,201 real respondents in 74 countries with personas answered by 12 model configurations from OpenAI, Google and DeepSeek. The models recover gender, age and cognitive-ability gradients 77.8 percent of the time but cultural differences only 48.1 percent of the time, explain just 1.3 percent of individual-level variance, do worse in poorer and less connected countries, and agree with each other far more than with people. We then turn to the computational Turing test, which pits nine open-weight models and five calibration strategies, from persona prompts to fine-tuning, against real users on X, Bluesky and Reddit. A classifier still spots the AI reply 70 to 80 percent of the time, emotional tone is the stubborn tell, instruction-tuned models are easier to spot than base models, and sounding more human comes at the cost of saying what the human actually said. Along the way we revisit the silicon samples of Argyle and colleagues, the optimistic Nature result of Ashokkumar and colleagues, the Global Preference Survey itself and Larooij and Törnberg's critique of generative social simulation, and close on what it would take before synthetic respondents can replace real ones in surveys, market research and policy evaluation.
